How Text Encoding Works — Full Explanation

Computers don't see letters — they see numbers. Before SHA-256 can do anything, your text has to be converted to a stream of bits via UTF-8. Each ordinary character becomes exactly 8 bits = 1 byte. The 3-letter word "SHA" becomes 24 bits. This bitstream is the raw input that every later step in the algorithm operates on.

What's happening with YOUR data
Why it matters
UTF-8 is a fixed, deterministic encoding — the same letter always produces the same bits, on every machine, every time. Without that determinism, two people hashing "hello" could get different hashes. SHA-256's whole purpose (verification) breaks if the input bits ever differ from what the signer used.

How Padding Works — Full Explanation

SHA-256 processes data in fixed 512-bit blocks. Almost no message is exactly 512 bits — so we have to pad shorter messages up to that size, in a precise, standardized way that's both reversible (the receiver can recover the original length) and tamper-evident (you can't quietly tack data onto the end). Three pieces are appended in this order: a single 1 bit (the byte 0x80), enough 0 bits to align the block, and finally the 64-bit message length.

What's happening with YOUR data
Why it matters
The 1-bit marker prevents any ambiguity about where the message ends ("hello" + zeros and "hello\0" pad to entirely different blocks). The 64-bit length field defends against length-extension attacks — without it, an attacker who knew SHA(x) could compute SHA(x ‖ y) for any chosen y. The length pinning makes that impossible without knowing x.

How the Message Schedule Works — Full Explanation

Sixteen 32-bit words from the padded block aren't enough — SHA-256 needs 64 words to feed into 64 rounds. So it expands the schedule mathematically using σ₀ and σ₁, two functions made of bit rotations, shifts, and XOR. Each new word combines four older ones: W[t] = σ₁(W[t-2]) + W[t-7] + σ₀(W[t-15]) + W[t-16]. Each σ is a non-linear mixer — by W[63], every output bit depends on every input bit.

What's happening with YOUR data
Why it matters
The schedule expansion is where the avalanche effect begins. Flip one bit in your message and W[16] changes — which changes W[17], W[18], …, W[63]. By the time the rounds run, the entire schedule has been scrambled. This is what makes "hello" and "Hello" produce hashes that share zero structure.

How the Initial Constants Work — Full Explanation

SHA-256 starts every hash from the same fixed 8-register state: H0..H7. These are not random and not chosen — they're derived from the square roots of the first 8 prime numbers (2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19). Take the square root, throw away the integer part, multiply the fractional part by 2³², take the floor. That's your constant in hex. This is called a "nothing up my sleeve" construction: anyone can verify the math and confirm there's no hidden backdoor.

All 8 derivations
Why it matters
If H0..H7 were chosen by a person, that person could (in principle) have picked values where they secretly know how to break the hash. By tying the constants to a publicly verifiable math fact (square roots of primes), there's nowhere to hide a backdoor. Every implementation in the world starts from the same 8 numbers — that's why "hello" hashes to the same string for you, NIST, and your browser.

How the 64 Rounds Work — Full Explanation

The compression function runs 64 nearly-identical rounds. Each round takes the 8 working registers A..H, computes two temporary values T1 and T2 from non-linear bit operations (Σ₀, Σ₁, Ch, Maj), and produces 8 new register values where A' = T1+T2, E' = D+T1, and the rest shift over by one. Each round also consumes one schedule word W[r] and one round constant K[r]. After 64 rounds the state is hopelessly mixed — that's the entire point.

Round 0 with YOUR data
Why it matters
The non-linear functions Ch and Maj make the round irreversible: given just the output A..H you cannot algebraically derive what the input was. To "reverse" SHA-256 you'd have to try ~2²⁵⁶ possible inputs — more than the number of atoms in the visible universe. That's what "one-way function" means in practice.
